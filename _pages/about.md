# Research Interest
To me, function is what defines a living system. Planets circle the sun with no purpose, but even the simplest life has one: to survive and reproduce. To do that, its parts have to do something, and that’s what we call function. Hemoglobin carries oxygen, E. coli swims toward sugar, and birds fly together in flocks. Living systems at every scale have taught us a great deal. From allostery to chemotaxis to flocking, we are starting to be able to say that we understand a little of this colorful living world.

But the world wasn’t always like this. About four billion years ago, nothing on Earth was alive. That raises a deeper and, to me, very mysterious question: how do these functions arise and persist? Is there a general principle, some underlying physics, behind all of them? On shorter timescales, the same question becomes one about learning and adaptation: how does a living system find a function and keep refining it?

In my own research, I focus on a modest system: the protein, the smallest biological unit with real function. I build simple toy models in the hope that they’ll offer some insight into learning and adaptation. The deeper I go, the more amazed I am by how many different strategies proteins have found to do their jobs. Some use rigid-body motions, some unfold locally, and some are intrinsically disordered altogether. I don’t design these solutions; I let them evolve. Using simple evolution-inspired algorithms, my toy protein discovers many of the same strategies on its own. That has given me a feel for how and why real proteins arrive at such versatile functions. It’s been a wonderful journey!
